How to read this

The stack shows every dollar of capital funding this company, ordered by seniority — the order in which each holder gets paid back if the company is sold or liquidated. Senior debt sits at the bottom because it's paid first and carries the least risk; common equity sits at the top because shareholders are paid last but capture all the upside if the company performs well.

Enterprise Value is the sum of every layer, minus cash on hand. It represents the value of the company's core operations — independent of how that value happens to be financed.

How to read this

When a company is sold or liquidated, proceeds are paid out strictly in order of seniority — each tranche must be paid in full before the next one in line receives anything. Senior debt is paid first, common equity last.

Equity has no fixed claim — it simply receives whatever is left over. That's why equity can be wiped out entirely in a low-value scenario, but also why it captures all the upside if the exit value exceeds total capital raised.

How to read this

WACC blends the cost of each layer of capital, weighted by how much of the total structure it represents. It's the minimum return a company's investments need to generate to satisfy everyone who has financed it — debt holders, preferred holders, and shareholders alike.

Debt's cost is reduced by the tax shield, since interest payments are tax-deductible — that's why after-tax cost of debt is typically lower than its stated interest rate. Equity has no such shield, which is one reason it's the most expensive layer of capital despite ranking last in the payout order.

How to read this

Lenders set leverage and coverage covenants to limit how much debt a company can carry relative to its cash flow. Debt/EBITDA and Net Debt/EBITDA measure how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay debt outright; interest coverage measures how comfortably EBITDA covers interest payments.

Breaching a covenant doesn't necessarily mean default, but it typically triggers a renegotiation with lenders, restricted flexibility (like a dividend block), or a technical default depending on the loan agreement.
